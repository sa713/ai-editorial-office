## executive summary

The evidence supports a measured article direction: AI tools can create team friction when they are used without task boundaries, training, review norms, workflow redesign, or shared expectations about quality. The strongest support is not for a blanket claim that "AI makes teams worse," but for narrower claims:

- AI output can be useful on some knowledge-work tasks and harmful or lower-quality on others.
- Users may over-rely on AI recommendations or polished outputs when system limits are hard to see.
- Low-substance AI-generated work can shift cognitive and verification labor to colleagues.
- Adoption without training, governance, feedback loops, or workflow redesign can create uneven practices and coordination overhead.

The evidence does not support universal, numeric, vendor-specific, or organization-specific claims for this task without additional sources or user-provided context.

## key findings

1. AI usefulness is task-dependent, not uniform.
   - Dell'Acqua et al. found mixed effects in realistic consulting tasks: AI helped on tasks within its capability frontier and harmed quality on tasks beyond it.
   - Research implication: future writing can say AI becomes a problem when teams treat all tasks as equally AI-suitable.

2. AI tools can increase downstream review burden.
   - The HBR workslop study describes AI-generated work that looks polished but lacks substance and shifts rework, clarification, and verification to recipients.
   - Research implication: future writing can use "polished but under-thought work" as a supported pattern, with caveat that the cited survey is self-reported and U.S.-based.

3. Overreliance and miscalibrated trust are known human-AI risks.
   - NIST flags over-reliance, automation bias, information integrity, and erroneous outputs as generative AI risk areas.
   - A 2025 review frames automation bias and over-reliance as active challenges in human-AI collaboration.
   - Research implication: future writing can discuss trust calibration without claiming all workers blindly trust AI.

4. Organizational adoption gaps can turn individual AI use into team friction.
   - Microsoft reports broad AI use at work alongside training gaps.
   - McKinsey reports that many organizations are still early in adoption practices such as workflow redesign, role-based training, feedback mechanisms, roadmaps, and KPI tracking.
   - Research implication: future writing can connect tool adoption to workflow norms, training, and governance, without implying a specific organization is failing.

5. Generic workflow scenarios are safe if clearly labeled as hypothetical.
   - Research supports pattern-level scenarios such as handoff quality decay, verification bottlenecks, duplicated prompting, unclear ownership, and unsourced AI summaries.
   - These must not be presented as real incidents unless the user supplies examples or sources.

## background context

The task is about team workflows, not AI capability in isolation. The sources suggest the main editorial terrain is socio-technical:

- task fit: which work should use AI and which should not;
- trust calibration: when people accept, reject, or verify AI output;
- review load: who checks AI-generated work and where that effort lands;
- workflow integration: whether AI is embedded in an agreed process or used ad hoc;
- governance: whether teams have training, feedback, usage norms, and escalation paths.

## confirmed facts

- NIST's 2024 Generative AI Profile identifies erroneous outputs, human-AI configuration risks such as automation bias and over-reliance, and information-integrity risks as relevant generative AI risks.
- Microsoft and LinkedIn's 2024 Work Trend Index surveyed 31,000 people across 31 countries and reports widespread AI use at work, including rapid recent adoption.
- The same Microsoft report says only 39% of global AI-at-work users had received company AI training at the time of the survey.
- Dell'Acqua et al. studied AI use on realistic BCG consulting tasks and found mixed effects: performance gains for some tasks and quality drops when AI was used beyond its capability frontier.
- HBR's 2025 workslop article reports a survey of 1,150 U.S.-based full-time employees and describes low-substance AI-generated work as creating downstream rework and clarification burden.
- McKinsey's 2025 state-of-AI report says many organizations are still early in adoption and scaling practices, including workflow redesign, role-based training, feedback mechanisms, roadmaps, and KPI tracking.

See `/tasks/TASK-0002/facts.md` for fact-level traceability.

## interpretations

- AI tools can hinder teams less because the tool "exists" and more because the work system around it is underspecified.
- A team may experience AI as extra work when quality control moves from the sender to the recipient.
- The main article can be useful without alarmism if it frames AI problems as workflow-design failures, trust-calibration problems, and unclear ownership rather than as inevitable technology failure.
- The strongest safe article position is conditional: AI helps when matched to suitable tasks and reviewed appropriately; it creates friction when teams skip those boundaries.

## assumptions

- "AI tools" can be treated broadly as generative AI and AI-assisted workplace tools during research.

## contradictions

- Sources do not say AI is simply harmful. The strongest evidence says effects are mixed and task-dependent.
- Automation-bias evidence is not uniform across all settings. Some studies find overreliance patterns; others show context-dependent or mixed effects. Future writing should avoid saying users always overtrust AI.
- Workslop evidence is useful but partly based on self-reported survey data and should not be generalized to all workplaces without caveat.

## implications for writing

Allowed:

- Conditional, source-backed claims about task fit, overreliance risk, workslop/rework, training gaps, workflow redesign, and governance needs.
- Generic hypothetical workflow scenarios, if labeled as generic.
- Calm framing: "AI can create friction when..." rather than "AI destroys teams."

Use with caveat:

- Numeric claims from survey sources.
- Claims about productivity or value.
- Claims about adoption scale.
- Claims about team trust or reputation effects.

Do not use:

- Universal claims that AI tools harm teams.
- Claims that a specific organization has these problems.
- Real examples unless user supplies them or they are sourced.
- Numeric ROI or productivity-loss claims unless Chief Editor explicitly allows and Writer preserves source limits.
- Vendor-specific comparisons.

## do-not-say list

- "AI tools are making teams less productive" as a universal fact.
- "AI replaces thinking" as a factual claim.
- "Most AI projects fail" without careful source and scope limits.
- "Teams lose trust because of AI" without caveat.
- "This happens in our organization" without user-provided evidence.
- Any real-sounding workflow example that is not sourced or explicitly marked hypothetical.

## research-informed angle options

These are options for Chief Editor, not final editorial direction:

1. Workflow-fit angle: AI causes friction when teams use it for tasks without deciding where it is reliable.
2. Review-burden angle: AI output can move work downstream when senders skip source checks or substance checks.
3. Governance-light angle: the issue is not AI adoption itself, but missing norms for ownership, verification, and training.
4. Trust-calibration angle: teams need shared rules for when to trust, verify, reject, or escalate AI output.
