# Editorial failure patterns

## Purpose

This file helps identify why a text fails in real editorial work.

It is not a full error list, tone guide, scoring model, or new theory. It is a compact diagnostic tool for naming common breakdowns and repairing them without taste-based arguments.

## How to use this file

Use it during review when a text feels wrong but the problem is hard to name.

Name the dominant failure pattern, explain how it hurts reader usefulness, then choose the smallest repair move that restores the reader task. Do not turn every pattern into a checklist item.

## Failure patterns

### Pattern: Fake excitement

#### What it is

Artificial energy added to make the text feel lively.

#### How it appears

"Залетайте", "не упустите возможность", forced enthusiasm, too many emojis, exclamation-heavy calls to action, playful phrasing that does not match the reader state.

#### Why it hurts usefulness

It makes the text feel less credible and can hide the actual reason to read, act, or care.

#### Typical source

Fear that a practical message will feel too dry.

#### Repair move

Replace the excitement with the concrete reader-relevant reason to act: what changed, what is available, what the reader can get, or why timing matters.

#### Do not over-correct

Do not make every internal message flat. Keep energy when it comes from real specificity, people, novelty, or stakes.

### Pattern: Institutional opening

#### What it is

The text opens with the institution's reason instead of the reader's change.

#### How it appears

The first paragraph starts with a law, committee decision, regulation, internal process, department initiative, or project history.

#### Why it hurts usefulness

The reader must wait to learn what changes for them and why they should keep reading.

#### Typical source

The author writes from the organization's approval logic, not from the reader's situation.

#### Repair move

Move the reader-facing change into the first paragraph. Put the law, decision, or process lower as the reason or constraint.

#### Do not over-correct

Do not remove institutional context when it is needed for trust, accountability, or compliance.

### Pattern: Answer delay

#### What it is

The useful point appears too late.

#### How it appears

The text starts with background, importance framing, definitions, or reflection before saying what happened, what to do, what changed, or what the answer is.

#### Why it hurts usefulness

Readers spend attention before knowing whether the text is relevant to them.

#### Typical source

Essay-mode, expert-first explanation, or habit of "warming up" before the useful part.

#### Repair move

Put the answer, change, action, recommendation, or reader-relevant signal first. Add background only after the reader has the frame.

#### Do not over-correct

Do not force a blunt answer-first opening when a short trust or safety setup prevents misunderstanding.

### Pattern: Decorative warmth

#### What it is

Warm language that does not perform a reader-useful job.

#### How it appears

Soft phrases, compliments, gratitude, friendliness, or "human" sentences that do not reduce resistance, build trust, clarify relevance, or help the reader continue.

#### Why it hurts usefulness

It takes space and can make the text feel padded or evasive.

#### Typical source

Trying to avoid a cold or bureaucratic tone without knowing what reader state needs support.

#### Repair move

Tie warmth to a function: acknowledge inconvenience, name the reader's likely concern, show concrete relevance, or remove the phrase.

#### Do not over-correct

Do not delete human texture automatically. If it lowers resistance or makes a difficult change easier to accept, keep it.

### Pattern: Forced inspiration

#### What it is

The text asks the reader to be inspired, proud, or motivated when the reader task does not require that response.

#### How it appears

"Вдохновляйтесь", "гордимся", "берите пример", "зарядитесь энергией", broad praise for talented or outstanding people.

#### Why it hurts usefulness

It replaces the reader's actual task with the organization's desired emotion.

#### Typical source

Confusing awareness or social usefulness with motivational rhetoric.

#### Repair move

Replace inspiration demands with concrete reasons the material is worth attention: what is inside, what is new, what can be learned, or why it matters now.

#### Do not over-correct

Do not remove pride or celebration when the text's real purpose is recognition and the evidence is specific.

### Pattern: Mode blending

#### What it is

The text tries to perform too many editorial jobs at once.

#### How it appears

It explains, persuades, teaches, entertains, reassures, announces, and instructs without a clear primary movement.

#### Why it hurts usefulness

The reader cannot tell what the text is mainly helping them do, understand, trust, or accept.

#### Typical source

Unresolved stakeholder goals or no dominant mode chosen before drafting.

#### Repair move

Choose one dominant mode and one optional supporting mode. Reorder the text around the dominant reader task; cut or demote sections that serve competing jobs.

#### Do not over-correct

Do not pretend real hybrid tasks are single-purpose. Keep supporting material when it protects the main outcome.

### Pattern: Emotional avoidance

#### What it is

The text ignores a predictable reader discomfort, irritation, anxiety, or resistance.

#### How it appears

A change is presented as smooth and obvious even though it adds effort, removes a familiar option, creates uncertainty, or asks the reader to trust a new process.

#### Why it hurts usefulness

Readers feel managed rather than addressed, which increases resistance and lowers trust.

#### Typical source

Fear that naming inconvenience will make the message sound negative.

#### Repair move

Name the inconvenience briefly, then connect it to the reason, support path, or next action.

#### Do not over-correct

Do not turn the text into an apology unless the situation requires one. Acknowledge the state and move back to usefulness.

### Pattern: Trust without evidence

#### What it is

The text asks for trust without showing why trust is warranted.

#### How it appears

"Ваша безопасность — наш приоритет", "мы заботимся о вас", "это надежное решение", "так будет лучше" without reasons, constraints, evidence, limits, or accountability.

#### Why it hurts usefulness

Readers receive reassurance but no basis for confidence.

#### Typical source

Substituting brand-safe reassurance for actual explanation.

#### Repair move

Show the reasoning bridge: what risk or problem exists, what decision was made, what changes, what limits remain, and where to get help.

#### Do not over-correct

Do not overload the text with proof. Include only the evidence or limits needed for the reader to trust the specific claim.

### Pattern: Generic engagement language

#### What it is

Engagement phrases that ask for attention or participation without a concrete reader reason.

#### How it appears

"Ваше мнение очень важно", "будьте в курсе", "примите участие", "поделитесь обратной связью", "не оставайтесь в стороне" without explaining what will happen next.

#### Why it hurts usefulness

The reader cannot see why participation matters or what their action changes.

#### Typical source

Using communication templates instead of naming the actual participation loop.

#### Repair move

State what the reader can influence, how the input will be used, and what happens after participation.

#### Do not over-correct

Do not remove calls for participation when the text gives a real reason, clear path, and visible consequence.

### Pattern: Inherited purpose as hook

#### What it is

The text uses the base purpose of a format, rubric, magazine, event, or product as the main hook for a specific material, even though that purpose is not new and has not changed.

#### How it appears

An issue announcement explains why the magazine exists. An event announcement explains why such events matter in general. An interview announcement says interviews help readers get to know people, but does not show why this hero, issue, topic, or release matters. The text replaces a topic-specific reason with the inherited purpose of the format.

#### Why it hurts usefulness

The reader gets no reason to open this specific material. The text can sound correct, calm, and human while remaining generic and easily transferable to another issue.

#### Typical source

The review focuses on tone safety, warmth, and anti-corporate cleanup, but does not check whether the draft carries the actual editorial angle of the current material.

#### Repair move

Return to the concrete change, topic, hero, release, or issue. Ask: what is new here now? Why does this matter in this material? What would disappear if the topic were replaced?

#### Do not over-correct

Do not force novelty where the assignment is only a routine operational notice. Use this pattern when the brief names a specific topic, angle, issue, hero, or release that should shape the text.

### Pattern: Dead closing phrase

#### What it is

The final phrase formally closes the text but adds no meaning, action, clarification, or useful impulse.

#### How it appears

"Новый номер уже можно читать", "Подробности внутри", "Читайте в новом выпуске", "Присоединяйтесь", "Следите за обновлениями" when the action is obvious, unsupported, or not specific.

#### Why it hurts usefulness

Such endings are mechanical. They often signal that the text has not found a real final movement and can weaken an otherwise precise announcement.

#### Typical source

The writer tries to avoid a hard sell but still feels the text needs a closing line.

#### Repair move

If a concrete action is needed, state it specifically: where to read, when, or what to do. If no action is needed, end on a meaning-bearing line that holds the angle. Do not add an empty CTA.

#### Do not over-correct

Do not remove a plain action line when it carries real operational value, such as a link, deadline, location, or required next step.

### Pattern: Context inflation

#### What it is

The text carries more background than the reader needs.

#### How it appears

Long history, definitions, process explanations, stakeholder context, or rationale before the reader-useful part.

#### Why it hurts usefulness

The reader must process material that does not change action, interpretation, trust, or acceptance.

#### Typical source

Completeness bias or anxiety that the text will look underexplained.

#### Repair move

Keep only context that changes reader behavior, interpretation, risk, trust, or acceptance. Move optional background below the main answer or remove it.

#### Do not over-correct

Do not cut context that prevents misunderstanding, supports trust, or makes a concept transferable.

### Pattern: Operational overload

#### What it is

The text gives too many actions, details, links, or conditions without hierarchy.

#### How it appears

Several links, exceptions, deadlines, contacts, steps, and side notes appear at the same level.

#### Why it hurts usefulness

The reader cannot tell what to do first, what applies to them, or what is optional.

#### Typical source

Trying to include every operational detail in one message.

#### Repair move

Group details by reader condition or priority: required action first, exceptions second, support and references last.

#### Do not over-correct

Do not delete operational detail that someone needs to complete the task. Create hierarchy instead.

### Pattern: Fake usefulness

#### What it is

The text sounds useful but does not leave the reader able to do, decide, trust, apply, or explain anything.

#### How it appears

It promises to "help understand", "raise awareness", "tell about important changes", or "share useful information", but gives no usable distinction, action, criteria, evidence, or next step.

#### Why it hurts usefulness

The reader spends attention and receives posture instead of capability.

#### Typical source

Confusing topic relevance with reader outcome.

#### Repair move

Define the reader outcome in one sentence, then add the minimum structure needed to deliver it: action, distinction, criteria, evidence, example, or stopping point.

#### Do not over-correct

Do not require an action in every text. A text can be useful by improving understanding, trust, acceptance, or readiness.

### Pattern: Reader-state mismatch

#### What it is

The text enters from a state the reader is not in.

#### How it appears

It explains basics to informed readers, skips basics for newcomers, celebrates when readers are irritated, reassures when they need instructions, or instructs when they first need a reason to trust.

#### Why it hurts usefulness

The text may be structurally clear but emotionally, cognitively, or operationally misaligned.

#### Typical source

The brief names the audience but not the reader state.

#### Repair move

Rewrite the opening from the reader's likely state: busy, skeptical, annoyed, unfamiliar, anxious, curious, or ready to act.

#### Do not over-correct

Do not stereotype the audience. Use only reader-state assumptions that change structure or wording.

### Pattern: Buried change

#### What it is

The actual change is hidden inside explanation, rationale, or announcement language.

#### How it appears

The text says a project is developing, a process is improving, or a format is renewed, but the reader has to infer what specifically changed.

#### Why it hurts usefulness

The reader cannot update expectations or decide whether action is needed.

#### Typical source

Avoiding directness because the change is small, awkward, inconvenient, or politically sensitive.

#### Repair move

Name the before/after clearly: what used to be true, what is true now, who is affected, and what the reader should do or notice.

#### Do not over-correct

Do not make every change sound dramatic. Be direct and proportional.

### Pattern: Artificial Concept Completion

#### What it is

The editorial system completes a raw idea into a more mature concept than the materials support.

#### How it appears

Governance, process, lifecycle, roadmap, pilot, metrics, roles, or solution structure appear without source support. Gaps are smoothed over. Interpretation is presented as part of the author's idea. The material starts to look too complete, too coherent, or too implementation-ready.

#### Why it hurts usefulness

It replaces diagnosis with design. The reader loses the real state of the materials and may mistake synthetic structure for the author's actual idea.

#### Typical source

Completion instinct, discomfort with ambiguity, usefulness overreach, or pressure to produce polished output.

#### Repair move

Separate observed, implied, and inferred material. Restore uncertainty where the source is unclear. Mark hypotheses as hypotheses. Remove invented structure. Ask clarification questions instead of completing the concept.

Detection questions: is this present in the materials? Is the editorial system analyzing or designing? Did this structure come from the author or the model? Can the claim be proven from the source material?

#### Do not over-correct

Do not ban all interpretation or recommendations. Keep bounded hypotheses and material-level recommendations when they are clearly marked and do not become a finished project.

### Pattern: Premature Solution Substitution

#### What it is

The editorial system is asked to help the author understand the state of an idea, but starts offering a ready-made construction, solution, program, or concept instead.

#### How it appears

Diagnosis quickly turns into a proposed solution. Roadmap, process, governance, metrics, roles, or implementation elements appear before the idea is clear. The system starts assembling the idea for the author. Recommendations read like a finished strategy. Conceptual gaps are automatically replaced with invented structure. The author receives someone else's concept instead of understanding what is unresolved in their own idea.

#### Why it hurts usefulness

It protects neither the idea nor the author. Authorship is weakened, unresolved thinking is masked, and the idea gains false conceptual maturity through hidden solutioning.

#### Typical source

Completion instinct, pressure to be useful, confusion between helping and replacing, hidden consulting drift, or discomfort with unresolved conceptual gaps.

#### Repair move

Return from solution to diagnosis. Expose the unresolved conceptual area. Replace ready-made recommendations with questions, hypotheses, options to test, or clarification directions. Separate observed gaps from possible development. Keep conceptual ownership with the author.

Detection questions: is the editorial system helping the author think or thinking instead of the author? Did diagnosis quietly become design? Would the author still recognize this as their own idea? Are conceptual gaps being analyzed or automatically filled? Was a solution introduced before the idea became clear?

#### Do not over-correct

Do not ban hypotheses, development suggestions, clarification directions, or next-step recommendations. The failure is not helping the author; the failure is replacing the author's conceptual work with a ready-made solution.

### Pattern: Defensive Diagnostic Drift

#### What it is

After correcting for Artificial Concept Completion, diagnostic analysis starts weakening supported conclusions and becomes defensive analysis.

#### How it appears

Repeated disclaimers; "requires clarification" in nearly every section; supported conclusions framed as weak hypotheses; uncertainty repeated instead of grouped; the text protects itself from error more than it helps the reader understand the situation; conclusions are weaker than the materials justify.

#### Why it hurts usefulness

The diagnosis loses operational value. Readers get caution without enough clarity to decide, prioritize, or take the next step.

#### Typical source

Overcorrection after Artificial Concept Completion, fear of unsupported inference, confusion between uncertainty and usefulness, defensive review behavior, or pressure to avoid hallucination at any cost.

#### Repair move

Match confidence to evidence strength. Keep strong conclusions strong when the materials support them. Consolidate uncertainty into focused limitation blocks. Remove repeated caveats. Distinguish weak inference from supported inference. Restore operational conclusions.

Detection questions: does caution improve honesty or reduce usefulness? Is the conclusion weaker than the materials justify? Is uncertainty repeated instead of localized? Would a reader still know what to do or conclude? Is the text analyzing or self-protecting?

#### Do not over-correct

Do not ban uncertainty or hypotheses. Keep previous diagnostic guardrails: do not invent governance, roadmap, lifecycle, process structure, or completion behavior without support from the materials.

### Pattern: Sketchnote → Infographic Drift

#### Description

The editorial system formally creates a sketchnote, but the actual result becomes an infographic, presentation slide, corporate one-pager, UI-like scheme, or other designed visualization instead of a handwritten reader's note sheet.

This pattern was added after the first production sketchnote run in `TASK-0017`, where a visual article sketchnote request drifted into infographic/SVG/HTML output.

This pattern belongs only to `visual_article_sketchnote`. It does not apply to ordinary meaningful illustration tasks.

#### Symptoms

- Strict grid.
- Identical cards or blocks.
- Perfect alignment.
- Symmetrical structure.
- Presentation-slide feeling.
- Corporate one-pager feeling.
- No visible handwriting.
- No loose liner drawings.
- No sense of visual thinking in progress.
- Weak or absent paper-sheet feeling.
- SVG, HTML, web page, or interactive artifact treated as the main result.
- The image looks designed for a report instead of noted by a reader.

#### Causes

- Artist Agent starts thinking like a designer, analyst, or marketer instead of a reader.
- The semantic layer is too weak or not anchored in approved `visual_concept.md`.
- `sketchnote_brief.md` turns into an infographic brief.
- Sketchnote genre constraints are missing, weak, or ignored during prompt execution.
- Visual constraints against grids, cards, corporate layout, SVG/HTML, and one-pager output are not enforced.
- The model chooses the safest familiar visual template: clean blocks, icons, hierarchy, and alignment.

#### Risks

- The result loses the feeling of a live handwritten article note.
- The image no longer creates the right kind of interest in the article.
- The reader perceives the output as an infographic or presentation asset.
- The visual branch stops doing the sketchnote job and becomes generic design production.
- Article meaning may survive as content, but the genre no longer matches the reader request.

#### Repair moves

- Return to the approved `visual_concept.md` and check whether the semantic frame is clear enough.
- Check the sketchnote genre constraints in `sketchnote_brief.md` and `image_prompt.md`.
- Remove strict grid behavior.
- Remove identical cards or modular blocks.
- Remove corporate one-pager, presentation, UI, or web-page language.
- Strengthen the handwritten note character: paper sheet, liner drawings, uneven spacing, arrows, labels, and reader-like visual thinking.
- Reframe the output as "notes made by an attentive reader", not "designed explanation".
- Treat SVG or HTML only as an internal intermediate if necessary; the final sketchnote result should be image output.

#### Detection questions

- Does the result look like a personal article note?
- Could an attentive reader plausibly have drawn this in a notebook?
- Does it look like an infographic, one-pager, report page, or presentation slide?
- Are there identical cards, perfect alignment, or a strict grid?
- Has the feeling of paper disappeared?
- Has handwriting been replaced by clean typographic labels?
- Are the visual relationships loose and reader-like, or designed and corporate?
- Is SVG/HTML/web output being treated as the final result?

#### Do not over-correct

Do not apply this pattern to ordinary illustration tasks. Some visual assignments may legitimately be infographics, slides, diagrams, or structured visuals when the user asks for those formats. The failure appears when a `visual_article_sketchnote` request loses its handwritten reader-note genre.
