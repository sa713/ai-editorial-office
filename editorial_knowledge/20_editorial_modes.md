# Editorial interaction modes

These modes describe how a reader interacts with a text and what editorial usefulness the text must provide.
They are not content formats or publishing channels.

## Editorial mode

### Reader goal
Choose between options, approve a course of action, or understand the trade-offs behind a decision.

### Useful outcome
The reader has a recommended choice, clear criteria, and enough reasoning to accept, reject, or adapt the recommendation.

### Typical reader state
The reader has partial information, competing options, uncertainty about consequences, or pressure to decide.

### Preferred structure behavior
Start with the recommendation or decision frame, then show criteria, option comparison, risks, and next action.

### Allowed narrative density
Low. Context is allowed only when it changes the decision criteria or risk profile.

### Review priorities
Check that the recommendation is visible early, criteria are explicit, alternatives are comparable, and assumptions are stated.

### Common anti-patterns
Listing options without decision logic; hiding the recommendation at the end; adding background that does not affect the choice.

## Editorial mode

### Reader goal
Complete a task correctly, avoid mistakes, or follow a process.

### Useful outcome
The reader knows what to do, in what order, under what conditions, and how to verify success.

### Typical reader state
The reader is trying to act now or prepare to act, with limited tolerance for interpretation.

### Preferred structure behavior
Use condition -> action -> result movement; sequence steps around execution, checks, exceptions, and failure recovery.

### Allowed narrative density
Very low. Narrative should appear only as warnings, constraints, or rationale for non-obvious steps.

### Review priorities
Check sequence, prerequisites, edge cases, verbs, ownership, and whether each step produces an observable result.

### Common anti-patterns
Explaining the system before the task; mixing rationale with instructions; omitting prerequisites, checks, or exception paths.

## Editorial mode

### Reader goal
Get oriented in an unfamiliar topic, situation, system, or change without being overloaded.

### Useful outcome
The reader understands what matters, how the parts relate, and where to go next.

### Typical reader state
The reader is new, disoriented, or aware of the topic but unsure how to structure it mentally.

### Preferred structure behavior
Start from the reader's current confusion, establish a small mental model, then map key parts and next paths.

### Allowed narrative density
Moderate. Context is useful when it reduces confusion, but should not become history or comprehensive explanation.

### Review priorities
Check that the model is small, hierarchy is clear, terminology is limited, and the reader has a next direction.

### Common anti-patterns
Turning orientation into an encyclopedia; introducing taxonomy too early; defining terms that do not help navigation.

## Editorial mode

### Reader goal
Understand whether to trust a claim, system, organization, process, or recommendation.

### Useful outcome
The reader sees the evidence, the reasoning bridge, the limits, and the basis for confidence.

### Typical reader state
The reader is skeptical, exposed to risk, responsible for consequences, or missing proof.

### Preferred structure behavior
Separate claim, evidence, interpretation, safeguards, limitations, and accountability.

### Allowed narrative density
Low to moderate. Context is allowed when it clarifies credibility, provenance, constraints, or risk.

### Review priorities
Check evidence quality, specificity, limits, unsupported claims, and whether confidence matches the proof.

### Common anti-patterns
Using confident tone instead of evidence; overpromising; burying limitations; relying on reputation or vague authority.

## Editorial mode

### Reader goal
Adopt a viewpoint, support a proposal, or become willing to act.

### Useful outcome
The reader understands the proposed stance, why it matters to them, and what change or commitment is being requested.

### Typical reader state
The reader may be indifferent, unconvinced, busy, or balancing competing priorities.

### Preferred structure behavior
Lead with the practical claim, connect it to reader stakes, address objections, and end with the requested action or belief shift.

### Allowed narrative density
Moderate. Narrative can create stakes, but must serve the argument rather than decorate it.

### Review priorities
Check that the claim is specific, benefits are grounded, objections are handled, and the ask is clear.

### Common anti-patterns
Motivational language without proof; broad value statements; emotional framing that does not connect to a concrete change.

## Editorial mode

### Reader goal
Notice an issue, signal, opportunity, or risk that was not previously salient.

### Useful outcome
The reader understands what changed, why it deserves attention, and whether any action is needed now.

### Typical reader state
The reader is not actively searching for a solution and may not yet see the topic as relevant.

### Preferred structure behavior
Surface the signal first, explain its relevance, establish scope, then give a watch point, action, or no-action conclusion.

### Allowed narrative density
Moderate to high, but only to create relevance and proportion; avoid drifting into essay-mode.

### Review priorities
Check novelty, reader relevance, proportionality, and whether the text lands on attention, action, or deliberate non-action.

### Common anti-patterns
Announcing importance without a signal; exaggerating urgency; ending with vague awareness instead of a clear reader posture.

## Editorial mode

### Reader goal
Understand a change that affects them and know what it means for their work, choices, or expectations.

### Useful outcome
The reader knows what changed, who is affected, when it applies, what to do, and what remains unchanged.

### Typical reader state
The reader may be surprised, resistant, anxious, or trying to assess personal impact.

### Preferred structure behavior
State the change and impact first, then explain timing, affected groups, required actions, rationale, and support paths.

### Allowed narrative density
Low to moderate. Rationale is useful after impact is clear; organizational backstory should be limited.

### Review priorities
Check impact clarity, audience segmentation, dates, responsibilities, unchanged areas, and support information.

### Common anti-patterns
Starting with internal rationale; hiding negative impact; using vague transition language; failing to say what the reader must do.

## Editorial mode

### Reader goal
Learn a concept or skill well enough to use it in a real situation.

### Useful outcome
The reader gains a usable mental model, sees examples, avoids common mistakes, and can attempt application.

### Typical reader state
The reader wants understanding, but may lack prerequisites or confidence.

### Preferred structure behavior
Build from known problem to concept, then examples, practice logic, mistakes, and application cues.

### Allowed narrative density
Moderate. Explanation and analogy are allowed when they reduce cognitive load and support transfer.

### Review priorities
Check prerequisite assumptions, example quality, concept-to-use bridge, and whether the reader can apply the idea.

### Common anti-patterns
Explaining theory without use; adding too many concepts at once; examples that illustrate but do not teach transfer.

## Editorial mode

### Reader goal
Interpret a situation through a specific editorial, strategic, or expert lens.

### Useful outcome
The reader receives a clear framing, understands its basis, and can use it to evaluate the situation.

### Typical reader state
The reader has facts or noise but lacks a stable interpretive angle.

### Preferred structure behavior
State the frame, show what it highlights, show what it downplays or excludes, then apply it to the case.

### Allowed narrative density
Moderate. Argument and context are allowed, but the frame must stay explicit and testable.

### Review priorities
Check that the frame is not disguised as fact, evidence is separated from interpretation, and limits are visible.

### Common anti-patterns
Opinion without frame; selective facts with no stated lens; presenting interpretation as the only possible reading.

## Editorial mode

### Reader goal
Explore a problem space, discover options, or understand possible directions before choosing a path.

### Useful outcome
The reader has a mapped set of possibilities, meaningful dimensions, and a way to continue exploration or narrow choices.

### Typical reader state
The reader is curious, early-stage, uncertain about goals, or not ready for a final decision.

### Preferred structure behavior
Map the space by dimensions, clusters, tensions, or questions; avoid premature recommendation unless a narrowing path is needed.

### Allowed narrative density
Moderate. Context can support discovery, but should not replace structure.

### Review priorities
Check that exploration has boundaries, categories are useful, paths are distinct, and the reader is not left in a fog.

### Common anti-patterns
Random collection of interesting facts; too many branches; hidden recommendation; no way to narrow or continue.

## Editorial mode

### Reader goal
Diagnose what is happening, why something failed, or which condition applies.

### Useful outcome
The reader can identify the likely cause, distinguish similar cases, and decide the next investigative or corrective step.

### Typical reader state
The reader has symptoms, conflicting signals, or incomplete evidence.

### Preferred structure behavior
Start with the observable signal, then provide differentiators, likely causes, tests, and next actions.

### Allowed narrative density
Low. Context is useful only when it helps distinguish causes or avoid false conclusions.

### Review priorities
Check signal clarity, cause differentiation, evidence thresholds, uncertainty, and escalation or correction paths.

### Common anti-patterns
Jumping to a cause too early; giving generic troubleshooting; mixing symptoms, causes, and fixes without separation.

## Editorial mode: diagnostic_analysis

### Reader goal
Understand the current state of a raw idea without asking the editorial system to finish, mature, or redesign it.

### Useful outcome
The reader sees what is actually present in the materials, what is implied, what is missing or uncertain, and which material-level recommendations can improve clarity without replacing the author's work.

### Typical reader state
The reader has early, incomplete, or loosely structured materials and needs an editorial diagnosis before deciding whether and how to develop the idea.

### Preferred structure behavior
Keep given facts, implications, and editorial hypotheses separate; mark gaps and uncertainty; diagnose weaknesses in the material; formulate recommendations as questions, clarification paths, or bounded hypotheses.

### Allowed narrative density
Low to moderate. Context is allowed only when it helps explain the idea's current state, uncertainty, or material weakness.

### Review priorities
Check that the diagnosis distinguishes what is given, implied, and inferred; preserves the rawness of the idea; identifies weak points and open questions; avoids presenting assumptions as facts.

### Expected outputs
Brief idea diagnosis; what is already clear; what remains unclear; where the material is weak; questions to clarify; recommendations for the materials; claims that cannot be made without confirmation.

### Boundaries
Do not rewrite the materials, complete the product, or design governance, pilots, lifecycle, metrics, roadmap, or an operating model. If development is suggested, state it as a hypothesis or direction for clarification, not as a finished project.

### Common anti-patterns
Turning a raw idea into a mature concept; replacing diagnosis with design; creating artificial maturity; hiding uncertainty behind confident recommendations.

## Editorial mode: author_concept_diagnosis

### Reader goal
Understand the current state of their own idea without receiving a completed concept, strategy, or solution from the editorial system.

### Useful outcome
The author sees what is already present in the idea, what is not yet assembled, what is mixed together or not distinguished, where boundaries, mechanics, or success criteria are missing, and which questions need clarification next.

### Typical reader state
The reader is the author of an early idea, partial concept, or unresolved intention and needs help seeing its internal structure before developing it further.

### Preferred structure behavior
Separate problem, intention, mechanism, audience, expected change, and success criterion; mark what is defined, undefined, mixed, or assumed; keep authorship and next conceptual choices with the author.

### Allowed narrative density
Low to moderate. Context is useful only when it helps the author recognize the idea's current conceptual state or a missing distinction.

### Review priorities
Check that the diagnosis addresses the author of the idea; distinguishes concept state from material quality; exposes undefined parts, mixed elements, weak boundaries, missing mechanics, and absent success criteria; avoids completing the idea for the author.

### Expected outputs
Brief diagnosis of the idea's state; what is already visible; what is mixed or not distinguished; main conceptual gaps; questions for the author; directions for clarification; cautious development recommendations framed as questions, testable options, clarification paths, or hypotheses.

### Boundaries
Do not complete the concept, design the solution, write a strategy, or invent governance, roadmap, lifecycle, pilot, metrics, or a finished program. Any suggested development must sound like a question, option to test, clarification direction, or hypothesis, not a ready-made solution.

### Common anti-patterns
Turning diagnosis into consulting, coaching, solution design, strategic planning, or program architecture; replacing the author's conceptual work with editorial invention; making undefined parts look resolved.

## Editorial mode: visual_illustration_brief

Status: inactive by default while the visual subsystem is frozen. Preserve this
mode as visual knowledge; use it only when `AGENTS.md` explicitly allows visual
subsystem activation for the task.

### Reader goal
Understand what visual task an illustration should solve for a given text.

### Useful outcome
The editorial team can formulate the main meaning of the illustration, what the viewer should understand in 3-5 seconds, the intended mood, a visual metaphor, required elements, constraints, and what must not be distorted.

### Typical reader state
The reader has brought a text and wants not just a beautiful image, but an illustration that carries the text's meaning.

### Preferred structure behavior
Start with the meaning of the text, then define the illustration task, viewer perception, visual image, and constraints; only after that, if needed, formulate a possible assignment for an illustrator.

### Visual meaning extraction guidance
Extract the main meaning the illustration must carry, not the topic of the text and not a retelling of the text.

Define the viewer takeaway as the understanding a person should get within 3-5 seconds of seeing the image.

Name the emotional tone the illustration should create, such as anxiety, hope, absurdity, tension, irony, calm, inquiry, or awe, only when that tone is grounded in the text.

Identify a visual metaphor that carries the main meaning, but do not accept the first obvious metaphor automatically; check whether it preserves the text's conclusion, tone, and complexity.

Mark distortion risks: simplifying a complex idea, turning serious material into a joke, making an aggressive image for a text about cooperation, or adding meaning that is not present in the text.

Keep this as a meaning-understanding stage. Do not specify composition, color, style, artist instructions, or final prompt wording here.

### Boundaries
Do not rewrite the text, invent a new idea, turn the task into an infographic unless asked, choose a banal metaphor without checking it against the text, convert the visual task into a presentation or comic, or generate a final prompt before a meaning brief exists.

### Visual anti-patterns
Literal illustration of every paragraph; generic stock image; visually completing the author's thought; beautiful image without a connection to the meaning; metaphor that changes the main conclusion of the text.

### Common anti-patterns
A beautiful image placed next to the text but not about the text; a literal banal metaphor; visually completing meaning that the text does not contain; changing the tone of the material through the image; prompt-first behavior.

## Editorial mode: visual_article_sketchnote

Status: inactive by default while the visual subsystem is frozen. Preserve this
mode as visual knowledge; use it only when `AGENTS.md` explicitly allows visual
subsystem activation for the task.

### Reader goal
Prepare a visual handwritten sketchnote of an article for publication next to the article.

### Useful outcome
The editorial team can read the article, prepare `visual_concept.md` as the semantic frame for the sketchnote, identify the article's central idea, map 5-10 key meaning blocks, determine relationships between ideas, identify the author's conclusions, define what the viewer should remember, capture the emotional or intellectual feeling of the material, and then prepare a sketchnote brief for an artist.

### Typical reader state
The reader has brought an article and wants an image that quickly explains the article's content, creates interest in the article, feels like a living human note, and does not look like a corporate infographic, poster, ad image, collage, photorealistic scene, or AI-generated visual mashup.

### Preferred structure behavior
Start by creating `visual_concept.md` from the article. For `visual_article_sketchnote`, `visual_concept.md` is the semantic frame of the article, not an ordinary metaphorical illustration concept.

The sketchnote `visual_concept.md` should fix:
- central idea;
- 5-10 key meaning blocks;
- relationships between ideas;
- author conclusions;
- what the viewer should remember;
- emotional or intellectual feeling of the material.

Only after this meaning layer is approved, translate it into `sketchnote_brief.md`: select what must appear on the sheet, describe visual hierarchy, suggest one-sheet organization, define controlled handwritten content, drawing elements, and constraints.

Keep the sketchnote selective. It should show how an attentive reader would summarize the article on one sheet, not retell every paragraph or turn the article into a map of everything.

### Output character
The visual result should look like one sheet of paper with handwritten text, liner drawings, arrows, frames, connections, underlines, small visual metaphors, and the feeling of attentive reading captured in a live note.

It should not look like a presentation slide, corporate infographic, poster, advertising image, collage, photorealistic scene, comic, or image with obvious AI-artifacts.

### Expected outputs
Primary upstream artifact: `visual_concept.md`.

Execution brief artifact: `sketchnote_brief.md`.

`sketchnote_brief.md` must be built from approved `visual_concept.md`, not directly from the article. The article remains the source of truth, but the brief must not reassemble the semantic layer from scratch.

The brief should translate the approved semantic frame into article meaning, 4-7 visible key points for the sheet, author conclusions, visual hierarchy, suggested layout, short handwritten phrases, drawing elements, forbidden distortions, and style constraints.

Artist Agent may use an approved `sketchnote_brief.md` to prepare `image_prompt.md`, but must not change key points, add conclusions, replace the sketchnote with an ordinary illustration, or turn the assignment into decorative image-making.

### Boundaries
Do not turn the article into an ordinary illustration, invent theses that are not in the article, summarize every paragraph, overload the sheet with small text, make an unreadable "map of everything", use fake handwritten text without controlled content, replace meaning with decorative scribbles, or convert the result into a presentation, comic, infographic, poster, or ad.

### Common anti-patterns
Ordinary article illustration instead of sketchnote; corporate infographic disguised as handwriting; dense unreadable note sheet; decorative doodles with weak article meaning; random fake handwriting; invented key points; image prompt that drifts from `sketchnote_brief.md` into a poster, collage, or generic AI illustration.
